A New Hampshire Paradox?

Exploring the unexpected relationship between wealth and cancer surveillance across U.S. states and NH counties.

Data sources: NCI SEER, CDC WONDER, U.S. Census ACS

About this project

Having lived in New Hampshire for a decade, it is fair to claim the perspective of a New Hampshirite. The state consistently maintains one of the nation’s lowest unemployment rates at approximately 3.1%1 and ranks seventh in median household income at roughly $96,000.2 These indicators place New Hampshire among the most economically prosperous states in the country.

Yet the Granite State presents a notable public health paradox: it ranks second nationally in bladder cancer incidence3, sixth in breast cancer incidence4, and first in brain cancer incidence5—figures that appear unexpectedly high given the state’s overall socioeconomic advantages.

This project evaluates this paradox by analyzing county‑level socioeconomic and cancer surveillance data. Through data visualizations, the goal is to determine whether this pattern reflects a true epidemiologic phenomenon or an artifact of surveillance. A key focus is assessing whether county‑level variation suggests a statewide “masking effect,” where higher socioeconomic status may drive increased detection.

While environmental exposures (e.g., radon, arsenic), demographic aging, and broader social determinants may also contribute, these factors fall outside the project’s current scope. Instead, the aim is to establish whether the paradox is observable within statewide surveillance data and whether socioeconomic and geographic patterns support the possibility of a masking effect. Clarifying whether these elevated cancer rates represent genuine disease burden or surveillance-driven artifacts has important implications for resource allocation, public health strategies, and interpretation of cancer data.

Research question

Is New Hampshire’s higher median household income (socioeconomic status) associated with higher cancer incidence rates from 2000-2022, providing evidence for a detection-based “masking effect?”

Companion study to the capstone

The Hidden Exposure: Arsenic in Well Water

Can geology and chemistry predict which New Hampshire wells have arsenic above a safety threshold?

The question Jump to the findings Back to the capstone

The capstone sets environmental exposures such as arsenic aside. This extension picks that thread up, because arsenic is a true exposure: it can raise cancer rates whether or not anyone is screening more, so it speaks to the surveillance question directly. It predicts arsenic in water, not cancer in people, and it can only suggest a link, never prove one.

The question

Can a well’s geology, geochemistry and construction predict whether its arsenic is above 5 µg/L, so that testing can start where it matters most?

Arsenic occurs naturally in New Hampshire’s bedrock. It has no taste, smell or color, and private wells are not required to be tested. A model that ranks wells by risk could tell us which to test first.

Short answer: yes, but only modestly. Logistic regression ranks wells with a PR-AUC of 0.33, against 0.18 for a model with no skill. At its cutoff it finds about 72 of every 100 contaminated wells, but only about 26 of every 100 flagged wells are truly above the threshold. That suits deciding which wells to test first, not declaring a particular well unsafe.

The data

The data are a US Geological Survey testing set for an existing arsenic model. It is small on purpose: well locations are hidden down to the county, a few geology flags are set for only one to three wells, and about a fifth of the raw arsenic values are missing. The analysis works with those limits openly.

The analysis, step by step

The notebook behind this page runs as a single pipeline, in four parts. Each step below says what was done and what it found.

  1. Part 1. Data input and validation
  2. 1

    Load the file and check it

    What was done. Read the file, checked that it is usable (readable, no repeated column names, a valid yes/no target, both outcomes present), and profiled every column.

    What it found. 373 wells and 50 columns, with 45 candidate predictors. 68 wells (18.2%) are above 5 µg/L. One warning: 1 column holds a single value.

  3. Part 2. Data processing and exploration
  4. 2

    Check data quality

    What was done. Looked for missing values, impossible values, extreme values, rare flags and constant columns, with each check aware of the column’s type.

    What it found. 78 of 373 raw arsenic values (20.9%) are missing, so the target uses the dataset’s own complete flag. 5 geology flags are set for fewer than 5 wells.

  5. 3

    Clean without cheating

    What was done. Dropped constant columns, merged rare flags, filled gaps with the median, standardized numbers and turned categories into 0/1 columns. Every step is learned from the training wells only, inside each fold, so the wells being tested never influence the cleaning.

    What it found. 45 predictors become 48 model-ready features.

  6. 4

    Describe and map

    What was done. Summarized every column, drew the class balance, distributions and correlations, and mapped exceedance by county.

    What it found. About 82% of wells are below the threshold, so a model that always says “safe” would be 82% accurate and find nothing. That is why accuracy is not the headline metric.

  7. Part 3. Analytical pipeline
  8. 5

    Test every predictor against the outcome

    What was done. Each predictor got the test its data called for: Mann-Whitney U for lopsided measurements, Welch’s t-test when both groups look bell-shaped, chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for flags and categories. All p-values were corrected for testing many predictors at once (Benjamini-Hochberg).

    What it found. 11 of 44 predictors survive the correction.

  9. 6

    Let the data set the plan

    What was done. The settings adapt to the data: the number of folds and repeats, and how many predictors the explanatory model can support (about 10 contaminated wells per predictor).

    What it found. 5-fold cross-validation repeated 2 times, and 6 predictors for the explanatory model.

  10. 7

    Build and tune three models

    What was done. Logistic regression (the interpretable benchmark), random forest and gradient boosting. Each model’s settings were chosen by grid search (5-fold cross-validation, scored by log-loss).

    What it found. A fixed primary metric was chosen before any model ran: PR-AUC, which measures how well the rare contaminated wells are ranked and needs no cutoff.

  11. Part 4. Model evaluation and interpretation
  12. 8

    Score every well on a model that never saw it

    What was done. Nested cross-validation repeats the tuning and the choice of cutoff inside each fold. Ten metrics are reported for every model, each with a 95% bootstrap interval.

    What it found. Random forest has the highest PR-AUC (0.38), but its lead is within the uncertainty.

  13. 9

    Select by a rule fixed in advance

    What was done. The model with the best primary metric wins, unless a simpler model is statistically tied with it. Then the simpler one wins.

    What it found. Logistic regression is selected (PR-AUC 0.33, 95% interval 0.25 to 0.45; a model with no skill scores 0.18).

Findings

1Where the exceedances are

Exceedance is highest in Strafford (41%, 16 of 39 wells), Merrimack (33%, 17 of 51 wells), Rockingham (26%, 17 of 66 wells). It is lowest in Coos, Carroll, Sullivan (5% or less). Across all wells, 18% are above 5 µg/L. A dark county with few wells tested could be chance, so check the interval in each tooltip.

2There is real signal, but a modest one

Logistic regression reaches a PR-AUC of 0.33 (95% interval 0.25 to 0.45). A model with no skill scores 0.18, the share of contaminated wells, so the score is about 1.8 times that baseline. The whole interval sits above the no-skill value, so the signal is real. Geology and geochemistry carry information about which wells exceed 5 µg/L, but not enough to be decisive.

3The simplest model wins, by a rule fixed in advance

Logistic regression is simpler than the leader (Random forest), and the gap between them (+0.049, 95% interval -0.020 to +0.116) includes zero, so the simpler model is preferred. With a few hundred wells the data cannot tell the three models apart, so a more flexible model has to be clearly better to win. Here it was not.

4What a flagged well means

At its chosen cutoff, logistic regression catches about 72 of every 100 contaminated wells and correctly clears about 55 of every 100 safe wells, but only about 26 of every 100 flagged wells are truly above the threshold. That trade (find most contaminated wells, at the cost of many extra tests) suits deciding which wells to test first. It does not suit declaring a particular well unsafe.

5The model ranks counties well but squeezes the range

It orders the counties very closely to the real rates (rank agreement 0.93), but it compresses them, predicting 9% to 32% where the real rates run from 0% to 41%. The Brier score, 0.138 against 0.149 for no skill, is only a modest improvement. Use the risks to rank wells, not as exact percentages.

6Which predictors matter

Of the 6 predictors in the explanatory model, 3 have an effect clearly distinguishable from none: rain7100mm (per 1 SD): odds ratio 0.45, interval 0.29 to 0.72; ln_nure_as (per 1 SD): odds ratio 1.60, interval 1.18 to 2.17; nh_sdrift1 (yes vs no): odds ratio 0.40, interval 0.20 to 0.79. 11 of 44 predictors survive the correction in the screening. The p-values here are exploratory, because the predictors were chosen on geochemical grounds after seeing the screening. One direction deserves care: nure_ph points the opposite way from the usual expectation that a higher pH mobilizes arsenic (odds ratio 0.74, interval 0.52 to 1.06). Its interval includes 1, and the data dictionary lists it as coming from the NURE groundwater survey, which may be a regional value rather than a measurement at each well, so treat it as unresolved.

7The models agree on 2 predictors

Each model was asked which predictors it leans on, with each model's top predictor scored 1.00. A predictor that scores 0.5 or higher for every model is one they agree on: ln_nure_fe, nure_ph. Others matter to just one model (for example popd00mean, ratiopbcu, nure_cond), which deserves less trust.

What this cannot tell us

  • It is association, not cause. A predictor that flags wells is not necessarily what puts arsenic in them.
  • The wells are a USGS testing set, not a random sample of New Hampshire wells, so the exceedance rates here are not the statewide share of contaminated wells.
  • It describes the water in a well, not any person’s exposure or health. A county-level comparison with cancer rates would be ecological and cannot show individual harm.
  • With a few dozen contaminated wells, wide intervals are the honest result, not a flaw to hide.
  • The wells were sampled in 2004 to 2005, and bladder cancer typically appears decades after exposure.
  • The explanatory plots (odds ratios, importance) describe this dataset. Only the cross-validated scores estimate performance on wells the models have not seen.

What comes next

  • A hierarchical model that shrinks small-county estimates toward the state average, which is the honest way to use counties with few wells.
  • A continuous statewide risk surface, once well-level locations can be used.
  • Putting county arsenic exposure next to the cancer and income data from the capstone, as a question about exposure and detection together.
